Entity Concept Violations in Accounting

Reading Time: 8 minutes Words: 850

In accounting, the entity concept is a fundamental principle. It states that a business is separate from its owners. This means accountants should only record transactions that affect the business. Personal transactions of owners should not be included in the business records. However, in reality, many companies break this rule.

One common example is when business owners use company vehicles for personal purposes. The company buys a car and records it as a business asset. But the owner uses this car for family trips and personal activities. The company pays for fuel, repairs, and insurance. These costs are recorded as business expenses. This is wrong because these are personal expenses, not business expenses.

Another example is when owners mix personal and business money. They might use the company bank account to pay for personal things. For example, they pay for their home electricity bill from the company account. Or they use the company credit card for personal shopping. These transactions should not be in the company accounts.

Some business owners put family members on the company payroll. These family members do not actually work for the company. They receive a salary but do no work. This is a way for owners to take more money from the business without calling it profit. It reduces the company's tax but is not correct accounting.

Another violation happens when owners use company employees for personal work. For example, they ask the company secretary to organize their daughter's wedding. Or they use company workers to renovate their home. The company pays these employees, but the work is personal. The cost should not be a business expense.

Business owners sometimes take inventory for personal use. For example, a restaurant owner takes food and drinks for a family party. Or a shop owner takes products for personal use without paying. These items are recorded as sold or wasted, but actually, the owner used them personally.

Another common problem is when owners charge personal travel as business trips. They go on holiday with their family but call it a "business conference." The company pays for flights, hotels, and meals. These costs are recorded as business travel expenses. This is not correct according to the entity concept.

Why do these violations happen? There are several reasons. Sometimes owners do not understand accounting rules. Sometimes they want to reduce business profits to pay less tax. Other times, they simply want to hide personal spending.

These violations cause problems. They make financial statements incorrect. Investors and banks might make wrong decisions based on these incorrect statements. Tax authorities might penalize companies when they discover these violations. Also, it becomes difficult to measure true business performance.

Accountants have a professional duty to follow the entity concept. They should advise owners about proper accounting. They should refuse to record personal transactions as business activities. Good accounting systems help separate business and personal transactions.

In conclusion, the entity concept is important for accurate financial reporting. While violations are common, they harm business transparency and credibility. Proper separation of business and personal activities benefits everyone: owners, investors, and regulators.

Key Vocabulary

  • Entity concept - Accounting principle that a business is separate from its owners
  • Transactions - Business activities that involve money
  • Assets - Valuable items owned by a business
  • Expenses - Costs incurred by a business
  • Payroll - List of employees and their salaries
  • Inventory - Goods held by a business for sale
  • Financial statements - Reports showing financial performance
  • Tax authorities - Government organizations that collect taxes
  • Transparency - Openness and clarity in business reporting
  • Credibility - Quality of being trusted and believed
