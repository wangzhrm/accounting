Accounting Principles in Practice

Analyze the following scenarios and determine which principle is at play.

1. Sarah, the owner of a small bakery, uses the business's bank account to pay for her personal car repair.

This is a violation of the Entity Principle.

The Entity Principle requires that a business's financial activities are kept separate from its owner's personal activities. Mixing funds blurs this distinction.

2. A consulting firm completes a project for a client in December but receives the payment in January of the next year. It records the revenue in December.

This is an observation of the Accrual Basis Accounting principle.

Accrual accounting requires that revenues are recognized when earned and expenses when incurred, regardless of when the cash is received or paid.

3. A company's management is facing bankruptcy and is planning to sell its assets. It values these assets at their liquidation value rather than their historical cost.

This is a valid exception to the Going Concern Assumption.

The Going Concern Assumption assumes a business will continue operating indefinitely. When this is not the case, such as in bankruptcy, assets must be valued based on their expected sale price, not their historical cost.

4. A business includes the high morale and work ethic of its employees on its balance sheet as an asset valued at $50,000.

This is a violation of the Monetary Unit Convention.

The Monetary Unit Convention states that only items that can be expressed in terms of money can be included in financial statements. Qualitative factors like employee morale cannot be objectively measured in monetary terms.

5. A large construction company begins a three-year project but waits until the project is completed to prepare its financial statements.

This is a violation of the Accounting Period Convention.

The Accounting Period Convention requires a business to divide its life into distinct time periods (like quarters or years) to prepare financial reports. Waiting until the end of a multi-year project prevents timely reporting.

6. Jane, the owner of a graphic design business, maintains a separate bank account for all business expenses and revenue.

This is an observation of the Entity Principle.

By keeping her personal and business finances separate, Jane correctly applies the Entity Principle, which views the business as a distinct entity from its owner.

7. A multinational company prepares all of its financial statements using U.S. dollars, despite having operations in multiple countries with different currencies.

This is an observation of the Monetary Unit Convention.

The Monetary Unit Convention requires that all financial transactions be recorded in a single, stable currency, allowing for comparability and consistency in financial reports.

8. A small business sells $500 worth of goods on credit. It records the sale as revenue only after the customer pays the invoice two weeks later.

This is a violation of the Accrual Basis Accounting principle.

The sale should be recorded when the goods are delivered and the revenue is earned, regardless of when the cash is received. This is a characteristic of cash basis, not accrual basis accounting.

9. A publicly traded company prepares a quarterly report showing its financial performance for the past three months.

This is an observation of the Accounting Period Convention.

By dividing its operations into specific time periods and reporting on them regularly, the company is following this principle, which provides timely information to stakeholders.

10. A healthy and profitable business records its office building at its historical cost, even though the market value has significantly increased.

This is an observation of the Going Concern Assumption.

Because the business is assumed to be a "going concern" (operating indefinitely), its assets are valued at their original cost rather than their current market value, as they are not intended for immediate sale.
