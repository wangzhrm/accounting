Liability Recognition Scenarios

The following scenarios test the recognition of liabilities based on key criteria: meeting the definition of a liability, relevance (existence uncertainty and probability of outflows), and faithful representation (measurement uncertainty).

Scenario 1: Product Warranty

A company sells electronic devices and offers a one-year warranty. Based on historical data, it estimates that 5% of products will require repairs costing an average of $50 each. The company has sold 10,000 units this year.

Should the liability be recognized?

Yes, the liability should be recognized. It meets the definition of a liability (present obligation from past event). Relevance: Low existence uncertainty (based on history), probable outflow (5% likelihood per unit, overall probable). Faithful representation: Low measurement uncertainty (reliable estimate from data).

Scenario 2: Pending Lawsuit

A company is sued for patent infringement. Legal advisors estimate a 40% chance of losing the case, with potential damages of $1 million if lost. The outcome is uncertain until the court ruling.

Should the liability be recognized?

No, the liability should not be recognized (disclose as contingent). It meets the definition potentially, but relevance: Existence uncertainty exists, probability of outflow is possible (40%) but not probable (>50%). Faithful representation: Measurement is reliable if needed, but probability fails.

Scenario 3: Environmental Cleanup

A manufacturing firm has contaminated a site due to past operations. Regulations require cleanup, and the company estimates costs at $2 million with high confidence. Cleanup is certain to occur.

Should the liability be recognized?

Yes, the liability should be recognized. Meets definition (legal obligation from past event). Relevance: No existence uncertainty, high probability of outflow (certain). Faithful representation: Low measurement uncertainty (reliable estimate).

Scenario 4: Potential Fine for Non-Compliance

A company may have violated safety regulations, but an investigation is ongoing. Experts say there's a 10% chance of a fine, estimated at $500,000 if imposed.

Should the liability be recognized?

No, the liability should not be recognized (no disclosure if remote). Meets definition potentially, but relevance: High existence uncertainty (investigation pending), low probability of outflow (10%, remote). Faithful representation: Measurement reliable, but other tests fail.

Scenario 5: Decommissioning Costs

An oil company operates a rig with a legal obligation to dismantle it at the end of its 20-year life. Costs are estimated at $10 million, discounted to present value with reliable models.

Should the liability be recognized?

Yes, the liability should be recognized. Meets definition (present obligation). Relevance: Low existence uncertainty (legal requirement), probable outflow (will occur). Faithful representation: Low measurement uncertainty (discounted reliably).

Scenario 6: Uncertain Tax Dispute

A firm is in a tax dispute with authorities over deductions. Advisors estimate a 60% chance of owing $800,000, but the amount could vary widely due to interpretations.

Should the liability be recognized?

No, the liability should not be recognized. Meets definition potentially, relevance: Existence uncertainty (dispute ongoing), probable outflow (60%). But faithful representation: High measurement uncertainty (wide variance in amount).

Scenario 7: Employee Bonus Scheme

A company has a performance-based bonus plan. At year-end, targets are met, and bonuses totaling $300,000 are calculable based on contracts.

Should the liability be recognized?

Yes, the liability should be recognized. Meets definition (obligation from past performance). Relevance: No existence uncertainty, high probability of outflow. Faithful representation: Low measurement uncertainty (calculable).

Scenario 8: Guarantee for Subsidiary Debt

A parent company guarantees a subsidiary's loan. The subsidiary is financially stable, with only a 5% estimated default risk. If default, liability is $1 million.

Should the liability be recognized?

No, the liability should not be recognized (disclose if significant). Meets definition potentially, but relevance: Existence uncertainty (depends on default), low probability of outflow (5%, remote). Faithful representation: Measurement reliable.

Scenario 9: Restructuring Provision

A company announces a restructuring plan, including layoffs. Costs are estimated at $4 million with detailed plans and communications creating expectations.

Should the liability be recognized?

Yes, the liability should be recognized. Meets definition (constructive obligation from announcement). Relevance: Low existence uncertainty, probable outflow. Faithful representation: Low measurement uncertainty (detailed estimates).

Scenario 10: Potential Product Recall

Should the liability be recognized?

No, the liability should not be recognized. Meets definition potentially, relevance: Existence uncertainty (testing ongoing), possible outflow (30%). Faithful representation: High measurement uncertainty (hard to estimate).
